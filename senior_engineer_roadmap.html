⌁ skill acquisition, not title acquisition

Skip the ladder.

"Senior" isn't a syntax tier — it's a proxy for having navigated enough real, high-stakes, ambiguous situations to be trusted with bigger decisions. You can't fast-forward through a title, but you can manufacture those same situations yourself, faster and more deliberately than a company ever would.

01

What "senior" is actually measuring

Not knowledge volume. Six behaviors that get tested under real pressure — every exercise below exists to manufacture practice at one of these.

01 — Ambiguity → system

Takes a vague problem and turns it into a working system, alone, without someone else defining the shape first.

02 — Ownership of trade-offs

Makes architecture decisions and lives with the consequences — including the bad ones.

03 — Legible code

Writes systems other people can maintain without the original author in the room.

04 — Restraint

Knows when not to engineer something. Says no to complexity that doesn't earn its keep.

05 — Composure under fire

Can debug a production incident while it's actively costing someone something.

06 — Translating risk

Explains technical risk to people who don't read stack traces, without dumbing it down or hiding it.

02

The variable that actually matters

Cycles of consequence >> years of experience

Someone who shipped one toy project a month for two years has less senior-skill than someone who owned three production systems end-to-end for eight months. Stop building isolated, disposable projects. Pick one or two systems and own them long-term — keep adding features, fixing the bugs real users report, handling the scale problems, refactoring your own earlier bad calls. That loop — living with your own mistakes — is where seniority actually gets forged. Prosnokorta is a strong candidate to push toward real users instead of staying a portfolio piece.

03

The skill stack to deliberately build

Six components. Click to expand. Each one maps straight back to one of the six behaviors above.

01

System design & architecture

→ behavior 01, 02 +
  • Study real-world system design, not LeetCode — Designing Data-Intensive Applications (Kleppmann), ByteByteGo, real postmortems from Stripe/Cloudflare engineering blogs.
  • For every project, write an ADR (Architecture Decision Record) before building — forces you to justify trade-offs instead of picking the popular tool.
02

Production ownership

→ behavior 02, 05 +
  • Actually deploy projects to real infra (AWS/GCP/a VPS), put real users on them, wire up basic monitoring (Sentry, UptimeRobot).
  • Get something to fail in production, then fix it live. One real incident teaches more seniority than fifty tutorials.
This is currently your biggest gap — and the highest-leverage one to close first.
03

Code quality at scale

→ behavior 03, 04 +
  • Write tests for risky logic — not 100% coverage theater, meaningful coverage where it actually pays off.
  • Set up CI/CD for every serious project.
  • Go back and refactor something you built 6 months ago — literally what seniors spend half their time doing.
04

Reading code, not just writing it

→ behavior 01, 03 +
  • Contribute 2–3 real PRs to existing open-source repos.
  • Forces you to read unfamiliar codebases under review pressure — a core senior skill juniors rarely get exposed to.
05

Mentoring & teaching

→ behavior 06 +
  • You already teach in public — redirect some of that instinct into technical writing (your TinyML/Edge AI roadmap work is a good start).
  • Answer on Stack Overflow/Discord, help juniors at university. Explaining something you half-understand is how you find out you didn't.
06

Business & product judgment

→ behavior 06, 02 +
  • For every feature, write down why it matters to a user or the business — not just how it works.
  • Seniors get trusted with decisions because they've shown they think about impact, not just elegance.
04

Where the stakes come from, with no employer handing them out

Freelance / contract

Real client, real deadline, real money, no senior dev to bail you out. You already have this lever — your e-commerce client project. Pick slightly-too-hard work on purpose.

Real users

Even a small niche tool. The moment strangers depend on your code, your engineering instincts sharpen fast.

Competitive programming

Sharpens algorithmic thinking, but doesn't build system-design seniority on its own — keep it as a side skill, not the main lever.

Unfamiliar integrations

Building with a stack you don't already know forces new architecture thinking — your IoT/Edge AI direction fits this perfectly.

05

The sequence

A rough 12–18 month arc, given where you're starting from. Each phase feeds the next — nothing here is parallel busywork.

Months 01 – 04

Take one project to real production

Deploy it for real, add monitoring, get actual users, write ADRs for the decisions you already made. This is where the production-ownership gap starts closing.

feeds → incident experiencefeeds → ADR habit
Months 03 – 08 (parallel)

Open source + a harder solo client project + public writing

Start contributing real PRs, take on a freelance project that's a stretch, and begin writing technical breakdowns in public.

feeds → code review repsfeeds → teaching reflex
Months 06 – 12

Build a second system, with something unfamiliar in it

Forces fresh architecture thinking instead of repeating patterns you already trust. Your IoT/Edge AI track slots in exactly here.

feeds → new architecture instincts
Months 10 – 18

Go back and lead the refactor of your oldest project

Apply everything you've learned to the system you started with. This closing of the loop is what separates "did a bunch of stuff" from "got senior."

feeds → the whole point

This builds the skill. It does not, by itself, get you the title.

Most companies still gate "senior" on a verified track record, not a portfolio — no matter how good it is. What this plan actually does is make you wildly overqualified for junior/mid roles, so you blow through those levels fast once you do take one, and it makes you genuinely dangerous on paths where nobody's checking your title anyway: freelance, startups, co-founding.
